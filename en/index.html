A Corpus That Outlives You, Not the Other Way Around

A bucket-based decumulation strategy engineered to pay ₹1 lakh/month from day one, step up every five years to defend purchasing power, and keep the growth engine compounding across a 30-plus year horizon.

In plain words — the whole plan in 60 seconds

You want ₹1 lakh every month, rising over time, for 30-plus years — without ever running out. The trick is to split your savings into four buckets, each with a job:

1Safe income

Government schemes & bonds that pay you every month/quarter — like a salary. Used first 5 years.

2The refiller

Slightly higher-return, still-safe funds. Quietly tops Bucket 1 back up each year.

3The grower

Shares/equity. Left alone 10+ years so it grows enough to fund your future raises.

4The safety net

A separate pot for medical bills & emergencies. You never touch it for monthly spending.

Each year you refill the lower buckets from the higher ones — and you never sell shares during a market crash, because Buckets 1 & 2 already hold ~10 years of expenses. You need roughly ₹2.5 crore for the income + ₹40 lakh for the safety net ≈ ₹2.9 crore total — all your own money. (You can hold part of it in a 60+ parent's name to unlock better safe-scheme rates sooner, but that doesn't reduce the amount — see §3, and mind the catch.) Everything below is the detail — and §7 is a tick-box to-do list.

Core Capital Sizing

🟢

In simple terms: Think of it like filling a water tank before a long dry season — you want it big enough that it never runs empty, even if some years get no rain. This section works out how big your "money tank" (we call it the corpus) must be on day one. Short answer: about ₹2.5 crore for the monthly income, plus ₹40 lakh set aside for medical & emergencies — so roughly ₹2.9 crore in all.

How much you must hold today. Modelled as a real decumulation — withdrawals drawn at the start of each year, the balance compounding at a blended 8–9% p.a., with a 20% income step-up every five years to track ~3.7% lifestyle inflation.

Why ₹2.50 Cr, not ₹1.79 Cr — the sequence-risk correction. The floor assumes a flawless 9% every single year and that ₹1L is a gross draw. Reality breaks both: (a) your ₹1L is net of tax, so you draw ~8% more gross; and (b) a poor run of returns in the first 5–7 years — sequence risk — can sink a portfolio whose average return looks fine. Run 1,000 randomised paths (calculator, §5) and ₹1.79 Cr survives ~1-in-5 times; ₹2.25 Cr about 3-in-4; it takes ~₹2.5 Cr to reach the ≥90% survival a retirement should demand. The bucket structure (§2) is what earns that back — by never forcing an equity sale in a down year, the real strategy beats this single-pool simulation.
Mind the total, not just the income corpus. The ₹2.5 Cr above funds monthly income only. A rigorous plan ring-fences a further ~₹40 L for health and contingencies (Bucket 4, §2) that must never be drawn for spending — so the total capital to arrange is ~₹2.9 Cr, all your own. Everything downstream — buckets, execution, tax, calculator — sizes off the ₹2.5 Cr income engine; the ₹40 L reserve is separate.

02

The Four-Bucket Architecture

🟢

In simple terms: Don't keep all your money in one place. Sort it by when you'll need it. Money for the next 5 years must be rock-solid safe (so a market crash can't hurt it); money you won't touch for 15+ years can be invested for growth. Each pot is a "bucket" — we use four, and each has one clear job.

Time-segmentation solves the core tension: near-term money must be safe, long-term money must grow. Buckets 1–3 form the income engine (₹2.5 Cr, split by time-horizon). Bucket 4 is a ring-fenced Health & Contingency reserve (~₹40 L) that sits on top — never drawn for monthly income — because the shocks that actually break Indian retirements are medical and one-off, not market. 03

Using Parents' Names — Borrowing Their Scheme Eligibility

🟢

In simple terms: The best safe-and-high-return scheme (SCSS) is only open to people aged 60 and above. Your parents already qualify — you don't yet. So you invest your own money in a willing parent's name to open these accounts now. To be clear: the parents aren't putting in any money — it's entirely yours; their name is just the key that unlocks the scheme. This does not reduce how much you need — you still fund the full ~₹2.5 crore. Its only benefit is better, earlier access to top-rate safe schemes — and it carries a serious catch, explained below.

Your own SCSS access is gated by age: the elder spouse qualifies only at 60 (~2 years away), the younger at 60 (~6 years away). A parent who is already 60+ can open it today, and each person carries their own per-person scheme limits. So putting part of your Bucket 1 money into a parent's-name SCSS does two useful things — but it stays your money, and it does not shrink the corpus you must fund.

1 · Access, brought forward

SCSS pays ~8.2% sovereign, quarterly — the best guaranteed rate available. You can't open it until 60; a 62- or 82-year-old parent can open it this week. Their eligibility lets Bucket 1's top-yielding layer be funded now instead of parked in lower-yielding substitutes for two years.

2 · Capacity, expanded

Every scheme cap is per individual. Each eligible parent adds a fresh ₹30L of SCSS headroom and a fresh POMIS limit on top of the couple's own — multiplying how much of the corpus can sit in the safest, highest-yielding senior instruments rather than taxable FDs.

So the honest benefit is yield and timing, not size: a slightly higher blended return on Bucket 1 and access years earlier — worth perhaps a few tenths of a percent on the overall return, not a jump in the success rate. If someone tells you routing money through parents "reduces what you need to save," that's wrong — the money is still 100% yours.

The serious catch — read this twice. The moment your money sits in a parent's name, it is legally the parent's, not yours — even though you funded every rupee. If that parent passes away, the money does not automatically come back to you: it devolves by their Will, or by succession law, and can legally go to other heirs — your siblings. A nominee only holds it; the Will decides who owns it. Only do this with: (1) a registered Will in which the parent leaves exactly this money to you; (2) correct nominations naming you on every such account; (3) a written family understanding; and ideally (4) you as a joint holder with survivorship. Also weigh the tax angle — income earned in the parent's name is theirs, and "clubbing" rules may apply — so run it past your CA first. If any of this feels shaky in your family, keep the money in your own name and simply accept the ~2-year wait for SCSS.
04

Risk Defences — the Gaps a 3-Bucket Plan Misses

🟢

In simple terms: A good plan doesn't only chase returns — it asks "what could go wrong, and how do we protect against it?" Here are the four things that most often wreck a retirement in India — big medical bills, living longer than expected, one spouse being left alone, and interest rates falling — and a simple safeguard for each.

The buckets defeat market risk. But the risks that actually break Indian retirements are the ones a pure drawdown plan ignores: medical inflation, outliving the money, the surviving spouse, and falling rates. Each needs its own defence.

🩺  Medical inflation (~12–14%)

Nearly 2× general inflation — costs roughly double every 5 years. Defence: Bucket 4's ₹25 L medical corpus + a base floater and ₹50 L–₹1 Cr super-top-up opened now (entry-age windows tighten after ~65). Budget for the 10–30% senior co-pay and the up-to-48-month pre-existing-disease wait — the corpus bridges exactly those.

⏳  Longevity past 85–90

A drawdown-only plan has a non-zero chance of depleting if either spouse reaches 90+. Defence: a joint-life, return-of-purchase-price annuity (LIC Jeevan Akshay ~7.5%, or NPS) sized to essentials only, bought in tranches at 60/65/70 (rates rise with age). Accept it's a nominal floor — no Indian annuity tracks 6% inflation — so keep equity for the real growth.

👥  Surviving-spouse gap

The 4-year age gap means the younger spouse (54) may manage alone for 6+ years — statistically longer. Defence: joint-life annuities only (never single-life); confirm health cover continues on the survivor; pre-set SWP mandates and simple written instructions so the survivor can operate the portfolio without you.

📉  Reinvestment risk

SCSS/FDs mature into a possibly lower-rate world, resetting income down. Defence: ladder maturities across tenors; use target-maturity debt funds / long-dated G-Secs to lock today's yield in Bucket 2; and buy the annuity floor sooner if long rates look attractive.

Estate & nomination — the ₹0-cost gap most people miss. In India a nominee is only a custodian, not the legal heir — nomination does not override succession law. Put in place: a registered Will for each spouse; "Either or Survivor" / joint holding on every bank, demat and MF account; refreshed nominations on all accounts, insurance and NPS; and a small liquidity buffer so the survivor isn't cash-starved during transmission/probate.

Execution & Withdrawal Blueprint

🟢

In simple terms: A pile of money doesn't pay you a salary by itself — someone has to arrange for cash to reach your bank every month. This section is the plumbing: exactly how ₹1,00,000 lands in your account on the 1st of each month, and how the buckets quietly top each other up so it keeps flowing for 30 years.

A corpus doesn't pay a salary by itself. Here is exactly how ₹1,00,000 hits your bank on the 1st of every month, and how the buckets refill each other so the machine runs for 30 years.

How the monthly ₹1L is assembled

1
Interest rails do the base load (~₹55–65k/mo)

SCSS & RBI bonds pay quarterly; POMIS pays monthly. Route every one of these to a single dedicated "Income" savings account — never your operating account — so cash flow is visible and auditable.

2
An SWP tops up the rest (~₹35–45k/mo)

Set a fixed-date Systematic Withdrawal Plan from the liquid/arbitrage fund (Bucket 1) into the same Income account on the 1st. This smooths the lumpy quarterly interest into a level monthly figure.

3
One standing instruction pays "salary"

An auto-sweep on the 1st moves exactly ₹1,00,000 from the Income account to your spending account. The buffer in the Income account absorbs timing mismatches.

Annual rebalance & replenishment — the cascade

↓
Every year, on a fixed review date, cascade downward

Bucket 3 (equity) profits → top up Bucket 2. Bucket 2 → refill Bucket 1 back to a full 5 years of spending. Cash flows down the risk ladder, never up.

✓
In good equity years, harvest

If Nifty is up strongly, skim gains from Bucket 3 to rebuild Bucket 1/2 — you're selling equity high. This is also your LTCG-harvesting window (see tax below).

✕
In bad equity years, DON'T sell equity

The whole point of the buckets: a market crash in year 8 doesn't touch you, because Buckets 1 & 2 hold ~10 years of spending. You let Bucket 3 recover and draw from safe assets. This defeats sequence-of-returns risk.

⟳
Refill trigger, not calendar-forced

Rule of thumb: keep Bucket 1 ≥ 3 years of spend and Buckets 1+2 ≥ 8–10 years. Only refill Bucket 1 from equity when Bucket 3 is at/above its target weight.

Tax strategy — minimise the drag (FY 2026-27)

🟢

In simple terms: The government taxes different kinds of income differently. Interest (from FDs, SCSS) is taxed like a salary; profit from shares is taxed less and gets a free allowance. Two easy wins: (1) India lets each person earn up to ~₹12 lakh a year almost tax-free under the "new regime" — so splitting income between husband and wife uses two such allowances; and (2) take part of your monthly money from shares, where most of what you withdraw is your own capital coming back, not taxable profit. Done right, a couple pays very little tax on ₹12 lakh a year.

The regime decision comes first — and it's counter-intuitive. The instinct is to chase the senior-only §80TTB (₹50k interest deduction) and the ₹3L/₹5L basic exemptions — but those live only in the Old Regime. The New Regime (default) instead makes income up to ₹12 L per person nil-tax (≈₹12.75 L with the ₹75k standard deduction, via the §87A rebate). For an interest-heavy retiree that blanket band beats the Old Regime's ₹3L + ₹50k for most cases. Your ₹12 L/yr income need, split across two spouses, largely fits inside two nil-tax bands. Run both regimes with your CA each year — but the New Regime is the likely winner here.

The calculation logic

Drop this straight into a spreadsheet or code. Withdrawals occur at the start of each year; the remainder compounds for the year.

// Parameters
C = your total corpus    // ₹ — you fund it all
Ceff = C            // income corpus
P = held in parents' names // ₹, a SUBSET of C (scheme access, not extra)
I = initial monthly income // ₹, net
S = step-up per 5-yr block // e.g. 0.20
R = mean annual return    // e.g. 0.085
σ = return volatility     // e.g. 0.08
N = horizon in years      T = tax gross-up

// Withdrawal in year y (y = 1…N)
W(y)   = I × 12 × (1 + S)^floor((y−1)/5) × (1 + T)

// Deterministic recursion (Bal₀ = Ceff)
Bal(y) = ( Bal(y−1) − W(y) ) × (1 + R)

// Monte-Carlo: repeat 1,000× with a random draw
r_y    = R + σ × randn()  // fresh each year
Bal(y) = ( Bal(y−1) − W(y) ) × (1 + r_y)
success = share of paths with miny Bal(y) ≥ 0

// Outputs
Sustainable ⇔ miny Bal(y) ≥ 0  ·  target success ≥ 90%
Required corpus = smallest Ceff s.t. Bal(N) = 0  (bisection)

On the Monte-Carlo: each of the 1,000 paths draws a fresh return every year from a normal distribution — mean R, standard deviation σ — so a bad sequence early in retirement is captured, not just the average. "Success" = the corpus never hit zero across the full horizon. Aim for ≥ 90%. This uses a normal return assumption (real markets have fatter tails and mild autocorrelation), so treat it as a robustness gauge, not a guarantee — and note the bucket structure is precisely what lets you ride out the weak paths without selling equity at the bottom.

About the annuity-floor slider — read this before you judge it. Move it and you'll see the success % barely change while the median ending balance falls. That is expected and honest: you're swapping money that could compound at ~9% for a pension that pays a fixed ~7%, and the pension doesn't rise with your 5-yearly step-ups. So on paper it looks like a mild drag. Its real value is the protection this model can't score: that income keeps arriving every month for the rest of your lives — past age 90, through a decade of bad markets, and without the surviving spouse having to manage a single investment. Think of it as insurance for your essentials (food, utilities, medicines), not as a way to grow money. A common choice is to annuitise just enough to cover non-negotiable monthly bills (₹30–50k), and let the rest of the corpus fight inflation.

Plain-English Glossary

Every term used in this plan, explained like you'd explain it to a friend.

Corpus

The total pot of money you've saved and invested. Your "retirement fund".

SWP (Systematic Withdrawal Plan)

An auto-instruction that sells a little of a mutual fund every month and pays it to your bank — the opposite of a SIP.

SIP / STP

SIP = investing a fixed sum monthly. STP = moving a lump sum into equity gradually so you don't buy all at one price.

SCSS

Senior Citizen Savings Scheme — a government scheme for 60+, ₹30 lakh limit, ~8.2%, pays every quarter. Very safe.

POMIS

Post Office Monthly Income Scheme — safe, pays interest into your account every month. ₹15 lakh limit for a joint account.

RBI Floating Rate Bond

A government bond (~8.05%) whose rate resets every 6 months. As safe as it gets, but locked for 7 years.

Index / Flexi-cap fund

Index = a fund that simply copies the market (e.g. Nifty 50), very low cost. Flexi-cap = a manager picks shares of any size.

Annuity

You give an insurer a lump sum; they pay you a fixed amount every month for life. Guaranteed, but usually doesn't rise with inflation.

Floater & super top-up

Floater = one health policy covering the whole family. Super top-up = cheap extra cover that kicks in above a set amount.

LTCG / STCG

Tax on investment profit. Long-term (held >1 yr) equity gains: 12.5%, first ₹1.25 lakh/yr free. Short-term: 20%.

New vs Old tax regime

Two ways to calculate tax. New = income up to ~₹12 lakh is tax-free but fewer deductions. Old = more deductions but lower free limit.

Sequence-of-returns risk

The danger of a market crash in your early retirement years — it can sink a pot that "average returns" say is fine. Buckets defend against it.

Monte-Carlo test

Running the plan 1,000 times with different random market outcomes, to see in how many it survives. We aim for 90%+.

DICGC insurance

Government insurance on bank deposits — but only ₹5 lakh per bank. Spread large FDs across banks.

Nominee vs heir

A nominee only holds your money after death; your Will (or law) decides who actually owns it. Make a Will.

Rebalancing

Once a year, moving money from buckets that grew into buckets that shrank, to keep the plan on track.
